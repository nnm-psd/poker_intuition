How a hand is played

Each player gets two private cards (hole cards). Five shared cards are dealt face up in the middle. Your hand is the best five-card combination from your two cards and the five shared ones. You may even use all five shared cards and none of yours, which is called playing the board.

  1. Preflop. Two forced bets start the pot: the player directly left of the button posts the small blind (usually half a big blind) and the next player posts the big blind. The first player left of the big blind acts first. The big blind's forced bet counts as a bet, so when the action comes back, the big blind can still raise.
  2. Flop. Three shared cards are dealt at once, followed by a round of betting.
  3. Turn. A fourth shared card, then betting.
  4. River. The fifth and last shared card, then the final betting round.
  5. Showdown. If two or more players are left, the best hand wins the pot. The last player to bet or raise shows first; if everyone checked, the first player to act shows first. Cards speak for themselves: what you hold counts, not what you announce.

From the flop on, action starts with the first player still in the hand to the left of the button. On your turn you can fold (give up the hand), check (pass, if nobody has bet), call (match a bet), bet, or raise. A raise must be at least as big as the previous bet or raise in that round, unless you're going all-in.

In no-limit, a bet is limited only by the chips in front of you, and there's no cap on the number of raises. Putting in everything is going all-in. You win a pot either at showdown or when everyone else folds, which is why bluffing works.

Amounts here are in big blinds (bb). A 100bb stack is the standard starting depth in cash games.

Hand rankings

From strongest to weakest. When two hands share a category, the higher cards win, and the remaining high cards (kickers) decide from there, but only cards inside the best five count. Suits never break a tie: identical hands split the pot, and an odd chip goes to the first tied hand clockwise from the button. An ace can be high or low (A-2-3-4-5), but a straight can't wrap around (Q-K-A-2-3 isn't one).

  1. Straight flushFive in a row, same suit. The ace-high one is a royal flush.
  2. Four of a kindFour cards of one rank.
  3. Full houseThree of one rank plus a pair.
  4. FlushFive of the same suit.
  5. StraightFive in a row, mixed suits.
  6. Three of a kindA set uses a pocket pair; trips uses one card plus a board pair.
  7. Two pairTwo different pairs.
  8. One pairTwo cards of one rank.
  9. High cardNothing made: the highest card plays.

On the flop you'll also hear names for how your hand relates to the board: top pair pairs the highest board card, an overpair is a pocket pair above every board card, and a draw is a hand one card away from a straight or flush. An open-ended straight draw has eight outs; a gutshot has four; a flush draw has nine.

Positions and blinds

The dealer button moves one seat clockwise after each hand, so everyone takes turns at the best seat. After the flop, the player on the button acts last, and acting last is a real advantage: you see what everyone else did before you decide. Being in position means you act after your opponent; out of position means you act first.

In a six-player game the seats, in order of acting preflop, are under the gun, hijack, cutoff, button, small blind and big blind. Later seats can play more hands because fewer players are left to act behind them.

Every hand in the test comes from one common spot: the button raises to 2.5bb, the small blind folds and the big blind calls. The pot on the flop is 5.5bb, and the big blind is out of position.

Equity, ranges and pot odds

Range
You never know your opponent's exact cards, so you think about every hand they could hold given what they've done. A big blind who just called a raise has a wide, capped range: most premium hands would have re-raised.
Equity
Your share of the pot if all the remaining cards were dealt with no more betting. It's measured against a range, not one hand. On K♠7♦2♣, pocket sevens (a set) have about 97% equity against the big blind's range; 5♥4♥ has about 20%.
Pot odds
The equity a call needs to break even: the amount to call divided by the pot after you call. Calling 1.8bb into a 7.3bb pot needs 1.8 / (7.3 + 1.8), about 20%.
Expected value (EV)
What a decision wins or loses on average if you made it thousands of times. Good poker is choosing the highest-EV play, not winning the current hand.
Equity realisation
Raw equity overstates what some hands actually win. A weak hand out of position often gets bet off it before showdown, so it realises less than its equity. Draws and hands in position tend to realise more.

Why you bet

Every bet needs a reason. There are four:

Value
You're likely ahead, and worse hands will call.
Bluff
Your hand rarely wins at showdown, but a bet can make better hands fold.
Semi-bluff
A bluff with a draw: you can win now, and you can still improve when called.
Protection
You're ahead but vulnerable, so you charge overcards and draws instead of giving them a free card.

Medium-strength hands usually check: a bet folds out the worse hands and gets called by the better ones. Strong players also balance their bets with a mix of value hands and bluffs, so opponents can't read their hand from the action.

Playing styles

Two questions describe most players: how many hands they play (tight or loose) and how they play them (passive, mostly calling, or aggressive, mostly betting and raising).

PassiveAggressive
Tight Nit. Few hands, rarely bets without a monster. Steal their blinds; believe their big bets. Tight-aggressive (TAG). Few hands, played hard. The solid default most players aim for.
Loose Calling station. Many hands, calls too much. Value-bet more and bluff less. Loose-aggressive (LAG). Many hands, lots of pressure. Tough when skilled; a maniac when not.

Adjusting to a player's leaks is exploitative play. The opposite is game-theory optimal (GTO) play, which doesn't depend on who you're facing. That's what a solver computes.

What a solver does

A solver takes a fixed situation: both players' ranges, the board, the pot, the stacks and the bet sizes allowed. It plays that situation against itself over and over, using an algorithm from a family called counterfactual regret minimisation, and each pass adjusts both players' strategies until neither can gain by changing. That balance point is a Nash equilibrium, the strategy people call GTO.

  • It answers for every hand. The output is how often to check, bet, call, raise or fold with each possible holding.
  • It mixes. Some hands bet 50% of the time and check the rest. Mixing keeps both lines balanced and hard to exploit, so either choice can be correct.
  • It's unexploitable, not maximally profitable. Against a calling station, the exploitative play beats GTO. GTO is the baseline you deviate from.
  • It only knows what it's given. The solves here allow two flop bet sizes and simplified turns and rivers. A commercial solution with more sizes can differ.
  • It's approximate. Exploitability measures how far a solution is from perfect. The solves in this test stop at about 1.5% of the pot; commercial tools usually aim lower.

How the test scores you

Each hand asks for two things against a running clock: your equity estimate, then your action.

Solver match
100% when you pick an action the solver takes as often as its main play. Mixed spots give partial credit.
Equity error
How many percentage points your estimate was off from your exact equity against the opponent's range.
Median time
How long you take to decide. Intuition is the ability to be right fast, so the bar turns red after 15 seconds.

Sources

The rules above follow Robert's Rules of Poker by Bob Ciaffone, a widely used cardroom rulebook: its hold'em, button and blinds, betting, showdown, ties and no-limit sections, and its glossary definitions of each hand. They're summarised here, not quoted. The hand order and the no-wraparound straight are the standard convention, which the rulebook assumes rather than spells out. Tournaments add their own procedures, set by the Poker Tournament Directors Association. Individual cardrooms can vary, so check the house rules where you play.

The strategy and solver sections describe common practice rather than rules. Their numbers come from this site's own solves with TexasSolver.
